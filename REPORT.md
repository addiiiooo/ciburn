## Sample construction

- Selection date: 2026-09-11T18:40:44Z. Method: for each of 10 languages (Python, JavaScript, TypeScript, Go, Rust, Java, C++, C#, Ruby, PHP) and 4 star buckets (100-499, 500-1999, 2000-9999, 10000+), the top 50 repositories by stars from the GitHub search API with `pushed:>=2026-06-01 archived:false fork:false`. The exact query strings are in `dataset/sample.json`.
- Selected 1993 repositories; scanned 362; 270 have at least one workflow file, 92 have none; 1631 were not scanned (scan interrupted or API error).
- Window: the 90 days before each repository's scan. Per repository: all workflow files, the file tree, up to 200 runs (the API's `total_count` for the window is recorded separately), and the jobs of up to 8 runs (newest completed run of each workflow first, then newest overall).
- Public repositories only, REST API only, aggregate results only. Job-level minutes are therefore a **sample**, not the full window; percentages are of the sample, and 90-day totals are labelled estimates.

## 3. How does per-job minute rounding change the picture?

GitHub rounds every job up to a whole minute. In the sample, 3,687 of 33,969 billed minutes (10.9 %) are rounding: time billed but not executed. Per repository the median share is 28.0 % and the 90th percentile 71.2 %. 52.7 % of sampled jobs ran under one minute and 76.0 % under two; every one of them bills a whole minute per leg.

## 4. What would the sample cost at private-repository rates?

At 2026 list prices the sampled jobs would cost $604.65; at the 2025 rates $795.94 (-24.0 %). Extrapolating each repository's sampled minutes-per-run to its 90-day run count gives a median of $5.73 per repository per 90 days, p90 $1,204.14, max $131,518.37, sum $253,514.48 (estimate: assumes the sampled runs are typical of the window).

Runner mix of sampled minutes: 96.2 % on GitHub-hosted runners with a known SKU, 0.8 % on GitHub-hosted runners with an org-defined label (unpriced), 3.0 % on self-hosted or third-party runners ($0 under the models in effect).

## 5. GitHub's "96 % of customers will see no change" statement

GitHub said (2025-12-16) that 96 % of customers would see no bill change from the 2026 pricing update. This corpus cannot test that claim: it sees no bills, no included-minute drawdown, and no private usage. What it can say, at list price and for the runners it can identify:

- 100.0 % of repositories with priced minutes have a lower 2026 list price than 2025 for the same sampled minutes; 0.0 % are unchanged (only arm64 2-core Linux kept its price).
- The postponed $0.002/minute self-hosted charge would have added $2.05 to the sampled minutes on self-hosted/third-party runners, 0.3 % of the sample's 2026 hosted list price (public repositories would have stayed free).

## Limitations and what this data cannot support

- Job-level data is a sample of at most 8 runs per repository; run listings are capped at 200 per repository (140 repositories exceeded it). Absolute minute totals are therefore not window totals.
- The sample is the most-starred repositories per language/bucket that were pushed recently, not a random sample of GitHub.
- Public repositories: their standard-runner minutes cost nothing on GitHub. Every dollar figure is a list-price equivalent.
- Larger Linux/Windows runners use org-defined labels and are unpriced here; macOS large/xlarge labels are priced.
- Recoverable-minute estimates are per-rule scenarios with stated methods; they overlap and are upper bounds where noted.
- Nothing here measures anyone's bill, included-minute consumption, or the effect of the postponed self-hosted charge on real accounts.
